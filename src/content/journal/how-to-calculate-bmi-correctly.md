To calculate BMI, divide your weight in kilograms by your height in meters squared. If you use pounds and inches, multiply your weight in pounds by 703, then divide by your height in inches squared. For example, an adult who weighs 70 kg and is 1.75 m tall has a BMI of about 22.9. BMI (body mass index) is a simple screening measurement that compares weight with height. It cannot measure body fat or show your overall health on its own.

This guide covers both formulas with worked examples, how to measure accurately, how to use our [BMI calculator](/calculators/bmi/), the standard adult categories, and the situations where the number can mislead.

## What Is Body Mass Index (BMI)?

BMI is a number calculated from a person's height and weight, expressed in kilograms per square meter (kg/m²). Healthcare providers and public health agencies use it because it is quick, inexpensive, and needs only two measurements.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) describes BMI as a screening tool that can indicate possible weight-related health risks. It is not a diagnostic test. Two people can share the same BMI and have very different muscle mass, fat distribution, and health profiles. That is why BMI works best alongside other information such as medical history, blood pressure, and lab results.

## How to Calculate BMI Correctly

There are two common formulas: metric and US customary. Both give the same result when the units are used correctly.

### Metric Formula (Kilograms and Meters)

**BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height (m)²**

1. Measure your weight in kilograms.
2. Measure your height in meters.
3. Multiply your height by itself.
4. Divide your weight by that number.

**Worked example:** An adult weighs 70 kg and is 1.75 m tall.

- Step 1: Square the height. 1.75 × 1.75 = 3.0625
- Step 2: Divide the weight by the squared height. 70 ÷ 3.0625 = 22.857…
- Step 3: Round to one decimal place. **BMI = 22.9**

This falls in the standard healthy weight range for adults. The category alone does not establish whether a person is healthy.

**If your height is in centimeters,** divide by 100 to get meters (175 cm = 1.75 m). You can also use this version directly:

**BMI = [weight (kg) × 10,000] ÷ height (cm)²**

### Imperial Formula (Pounds and Inches)

**BMI = [weight (lb) × 703] ÷ height (in)²**

The number 703 is a conversion factor that lets the formula work with pounds and inches. If you know your height in feet and inches, first convert it to total inches: multiply the feet by 12, then add the remaining inches. For example, 5 feet 6 inches = (5 × 12) + 6 = 66 inches.

**Worked example:** An adult weighs 160 lb and is 5 feet 6 inches tall.

- Step 1: Square the height in inches. 66 × 66 = 4,356
- Step 2: Multiply the weight by 703. 160 × 703 = 112,480
- Step 3: Divide. 112,480 ÷ 4,356 ≈ 25.8

**BMI = 25.8.** Under the standard adult classification this falls in the overweight range. It does not show how much body fat the person has or whether they have any medical condition.

## How to Measure Height and Weight Accurately

Small measurement errors can move a result across a category boundary, especially if you are near a cutoff. For consistent measurements:

- Use a reliable scale on a firm, level surface.
- Weigh yourself at a similar time of day, in light clothing.
- Measure height without shoes, standing upright against a wall.
- Use centimeters and kilograms in our calculator, and convert feet and inches to total inches only if you are calculating by hand with the pounds-and-inches formula.
- Recheck if the result seems unexpected.

Body weight naturally varies from day to day, so a single measurement is a snapshot, not a verdict.

## Adult BMI Categories

The CDC uses these categories for adults aged 20 and older:

| BMI (kg/m²) | Category |
| --- | --- |
| Below 18.5 | Underweight |
| 18.5 to less than 25 | Healthy weight |
| 25 to less than 30 | Overweight |
| 30 or higher | Obesity |

The obesity range is divided further into classes:

| Class | BMI |
| --- | --- |
| Class 1 | 30 to less than 35 |
| Class 2 | 35 to less than 40 |
| Class 3 | 40 or higher |

These are screening classifications, not individual diagnoses. They help identify when further assessment by a healthcare professional may be appropriate. The cutoffs are the same for men and women.

### What Each Category Means

**Underweight:** Relatively low weight for height. A low BMI does not explain its cause, so medical history and nutrition status may matter.

**Healthy weight:** The label describes a BMI range. It does not guarantee good physical or metabolic health.

**Overweight:** Weight above the standard healthy range for height. It may be associated with higher health risk, but BMI cannot show whether extra weight is mostly fat or muscle.

**Obesity:** Higher BMI levels are associated with greater risk of conditions such as type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and high blood pressure. A healthcare professional can assess an individual's actual health status.

## What Your BMI Does and Does Not Tell You

BMI can help you:

- See which standard adult category your weight and height fall into.
- Notice when a conversation with a healthcare professional may be worthwhile.
- Track general changes in weight relative to height over time.

It cannot tell you how much body fat you have, where that fat is stored, or whether your blood pressure, blood sugar, or cholesterol are in a healthy range. It also does not measure fitness.

## Limitations: Who BMI Does Not Apply To

BMI uses only height and weight, so it has important limits.

**Athletes and muscular adults.** Muscle adds to body weight. A person with a lot of muscle can have a BMI in the overweight or obesity range while carrying relatively little body fat.

**Older adults.** Body composition changes with age. Someone may lose muscle while their BMI barely changes, so BMI may not reflect strength or nutritional status.

**Pregnancy.** Weight changes are expected, so standard BMI interpretation is not suitable for monitoring weight during pregnancy. Clinicians usually consider pre-pregnancy BMI and other factors.

**Children and teens.** Adult categories should not be used for people under 20. The CDC interprets BMI for ages 2 through 19 using age- and sex-specific percentiles that account for growth.

**Certain medical conditions.** Fluid retention, some treatments, and unexplained weight changes can affect weight without reflecting body fat. These situations call for a broader assessment.

**People with a history of eating disorders.** Focusing on a single number can be unhelpful. A qualified professional can advise on appropriate monitoring.

**Different populations.** The World Health Organization (WHO) has noted that some Asian populations may face weight-related health risks at lower BMI values than the standard cutoffs suggest, and some clinical guidelines use different thresholds for specific groups. There is no single alternative chart that fits everyone, so interpretation depends on the relevant guidance and personal circumstances.

## BMI vs. Body Fat Percentage

BMI compares total weight with height. Body fat percentage estimates how much of your weight is fat.

For example, a person weighing 80 kg with 20% body fat carries about 16 kg of fat (80 × 0.20). BMI cannot show this, because it does not separate fat from muscle, bone, and water.

Body fat can be estimated by skinfold measurements, bioelectrical impedance, and dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA). Each method has limitations, and results vary with equipment and conditions. You can explore this further with our [body fat percentage calculator](/calculators/body-fat/).

## Frequently Asked Questions About BMI

### How do I calculate my BMI at home?

Divide your weight in kilograms by your height in meters squared. With pounds and inches, multiply your weight in pounds by 703 and divide by your height in inches squared. You can also use our [BMI calculator](/calculators/bmi/).

### What is a normal BMI for adults?

Under CDC classifications, the healthy weight range for adults aged 20 and older is 18.5 to less than 25. This is a screening range, not a guarantee of health.

### Is BMI calculated differently for men and women?

No. The formula and the adult CDC cutoffs are the same. People with the same BMI can still differ in body fat percentage and fat distribution.

### Can someone have a high BMI without high body fat?

Yes. BMI counts all body components, so people with substantial muscle mass can have a higher BMI without a correspondingly high body fat percentage.

### Is BMI the same as body fat percentage?

No. BMI uses height and weight. Body fat percentage estimates the share of weight that is fat. Neither is a complete health assessment.

### Is BMI enough to tell whether I am healthy?

No. BMI is one screening measure. A fuller evaluation may include medical history, blood pressure, lab results, and body composition.

## The Bottom Line

Calculating BMI is straightforward: divide weight in kilograms by height in meters squared, or use the pounds-and-inches formula. The result is a standardized way to compare weight with height, but it does not directly measure body fat or determine overall health. Treat it as a starting point, interpret it in context, and talk with a healthcare professional if you have questions about your results.
